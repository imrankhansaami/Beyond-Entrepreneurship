# 01 · The Framework — How an Enduring Great Company Works

*The model underneath everything: what "great" means, the twelve-principle path to get there, the six operating areas where the work happens, and how the pieces fit together for a small or mid-sized company.*

*Sources: BE 2.0 (Collins & Lazier, 2020; original 1992), The Map: Twelve Questions (Jim Collins, 2022), the Collins/Porras Vision Framework (2001), the Good to Great Diagnostic Tool (2006), and the wider Collins research canon. See §6 for the full reading map.*

---

## 1. The destination: what "great" actually means

The original 1992 definition of a great company had four criteria: **performance** (profitable, self-sustaining, recovers from setbacks), **impact** (a leadership role in shaping its industry), **reputation** (admired outside its walls, used as a role model), and **longevity** (staying power across decades — think 100 years). Jim Collins later condensed the destination into three outputs. Use these as the definition you hold your company against:

**1. Superior results.** In business, performance is financial (return on invested capital) achieved *and* progress toward the company's purpose. You must win at your chosen game. If you're a sports team, you must win championships — effort alone is not greatness.

**2. Distinctive impact.** A great company makes such a unique contribution that if it disappeared, it would leave a hole nothing else could easily fill. Practical test: ***"If my company went away tomorrow, who would miss it, and why?"*** Big does not equal great, and great does not equal big — a small, excellent company with a local market can be great.

**3. Lasting endurance.** The company prospers beyond any single leader, idea, market cycle, or lucky break; when hit with setbacks, it bounces back stronger. Practical test: ***"If I were completely out of the picture for a year, would it still perform — and would it still be building?"*** If the organization cannot be great without you, it is not yet truly great.

### The stop rule

Never treat greatness as a place you arrive. From The Map:

> "Greatness is an inherently dynamic process, not an endpoint. The moment you think of yourself as great, your slide toward mediocrity will have already begun."

Your job is not to *be* great this year; it is to *keep the flywheel turning* — and to make the company a little more self-propelling every year.

## 2. The path: The Map — four stages, twelve principles, one multiplier

The Map integrates Collins' entire body of research into one sequence. The stages build on each other: you cannot skip disciplined people to get to disciplined thought, and thought without action produces nothing. Return on luck then multiplies whatever you've built.

| # | Stage | Principle | Your recurring reflection |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Disciplined People | **Cultivate Level 5 Leadership** | Where am I/are we on the journey to Level 5? |
| 2 | Disciplined People | **First Who, Then What** | Do we have the right people on the bus, in the key seats? |
| 3 | Disciplined Thought | **Embrace the Genius of the AND** | Where must we replace the tyranny of the OR? |
| 4 | Disciplined Thought | **Confront the Brutal Facts** (live the Stockdale Paradox) | What are our brutal facts, and do we hold unwavering faith *and* face them? |
| 5 | Disciplined Thought | **Clarify a Hedgehog Concept** | Do our decisions sit inside the three circles? |
| 6 | Disciplined Action | **Build momentum by turning The Flywheel** | What is our flywheel, and how far can it go? |
| 7 | Disciplined Action | **Achieve breakthrough with 20-Mile March discipline** | Are we hitting the march in good times *and* bad? |
| 8 | Disciplined Action | **Renew and extend via Fire Bullets, then Cannonballs** | What bullets should we fire now; where are we ready for a cannonball? |
| 9 | Building to Last | **Practice Productive Paranoia** (avoid the 5 stages of decline) | Where should our paranoia be channeled? |
| 10 | Building to Last | **Do more Clock Building, less time telling** | Have we shifted from a founder-driven company to a system-driven one? |
| 11 | Building to Last | **Preserve the Core / Stimulate Progress** | Are we holding values constant while changing everything else — and do we need the next BHAG? |
| 12 | Multiplier | **Get a high Return on Luck** | Whether good luck or bad — what is our return on it, and how do we raise it? |

### The twelve principles, briefly

**1. Level 5 Leadership.** Build leaders — starting with yourself — who blend **personal humility** with **professional will**: ambitious first for the cause, the company, the work — never for themselves. Quiet, self-effacing, and ferociously determined is a legitimate (and common) profile; charisma is optional and often a warning sign. Level 5 is a *journey*, not a personality test.

**2. First Who, Then What.** Get the right people on the bus, the wrong people off, and the right people in the **key seats** *before* choosing the route. A specific business idea is likely to fail or change; the right people are the hedge that lets you adapt to whatever happens. There is one metric to obsess over: **the percentage of key seats filled with the right people — target 90% or higher.**

**3. Genius of the AND.** Replace "or" with "and" wherever the smart answer is both: freedom *and* responsibility; discipline *and* entrepreneurship; rigor *and* creativity; continuity *and* change. Small companies break this constantly — cutting training to "save time," dropping standards to "move fast," chasing growth *or* margin. Greatness lives in the tension.

**4. Brutal Facts (the Stockdale Paradox).** Retain **unwavering faith that you will prevail in the end, AND at the same time** the discipline to confront the most brutal facts of your current reality. Pessimists and unrealistic optimists both fail; the people who made it through the worst (Admiral Stockdale's years as a prisoner of war) were, strangely, both brutally honest and confident at once. Create a climate where the truth is heard — autopsies without blame — and where "it is my opinion" is never an acceptable argument.

**5. The Hedgehog Concept.** Find the simple, crystalline concept at the intersection of three circles:
   - What you are **deeply passionate** about,
   - What you can be the **best in the world** at (the "world" can be your market, region, or niche),
   - What best drives your **economic engine** (know your one denominator: profit per customer, per store, per route, per project…).
   Discipline means saying "no thank you" to big opportunities outside the three circles. Stick to your Hedgehog and you'll have "indigestion of too much opportunity," not starvation.

**6. The Flywheel.** Breakthroughs are not single events — no killer product, no grand program, no savior hire. Greatness is relentlessly pushing in one direction, turn upon turn, until momentum becomes unstoppable. Write your flywheel down as a chain: *step → step → step → step → back to the first step.* Then keep pushing. The enemy is the **doom loop**: lurching 0-to-100 in a new direction, stopping, and starting over.

**7. 20-Mile March.** Set a concrete, measurable performance marker — your "march" — and hit it in **both** good times and bad (the discipline of the original 20-mile march is that you hold the pace regardless of the weather; some marches have been held for 40+ years in companies Collins studied; you can set your own — revenue per person, delivery time, customer retention, margin floor). It provides self-control in chaos and restraint in boom times. Two failure modes: March of Death (too extreme) and erratic sprint-or-stall behavior.

**8. Fire Bullets, then Cannonballs.** In uncertainty, **fire bullets**: low-cost, low-risk tip-of-the-spear experiments that give empirical evidence. Only when a bullet hits, concentrate — fire a **cannonball** (the big, bet-the-company commitment). Intel's founders fired three cheap design bullets before betting on the 1103 chip. Bullets without calibration are just noise; cannonballs without bullets are just gambling.

**9. Productive Paranoia.** Assume the unexpected will happen, and channel your worry into preparation: cash reserves ("never leave yourself open to catastrophe; keep your eyes on the cash flow"), both zoom-out (long-term, wide) and zoom-in (current conditions) discipline, and a paranoid vigilance against the **five stages of decline**: (1) hubris born of success, (2) undisciplined pursuit of *more*, (3) denial of risk and peril, (4) grasping for salvation, (5) capitulation to irrelevance or death. Decline is a *choice*, and every stage is reversible — if it is caught early.

**10. Clock Building, not Time Telling.** Don't be the genius who tells time (solves today's problems yourself); be the architect who builds a clock (a system that always tells the time, whether you are in the building or not). This is the hardest shift for a founder, and the only one that produces endurance. Build mechanisms — not charisma, not personal intervention — that make your company work. Your measurement: how well the company performs in the hands of people who come *after* you (and whether you've made yourself unnecessary enough to take a real holiday).

**11. Preserve the Core / Stimulate Progress.** The master duality: hold **core values and purpose constant** (for a century) while **changing everything else** — practices, strategies, products, structures — with abandon. And keep the organization hungry: when the current BHAG is reached, set the next one. The alternative is the "we've arrived" syndrome, which turns energy into complacency.

**12. Return on Luck.** Luck — good and bad — is a factor you cannot control: it must satisfy three tests (you didn't cause it, it has significant consequences, it surprises you). Great companies do not get more luck than the mediocre; they get a **higher return on it**. Collins estimates that roughly half of great leadership is what you do with the unexpected. Tommy Caldwell spent 2,801 days on the Dawn Wall before the good luck — weather and cameras — arrived. Persist with the company, never with a single idea; and when the surprise hits, ask "what is this an opportunity for?", not "why me?"

## 3. The six operating areas — where the work actually happens

The Map says *what* the path is. The six areas say *where you do the work and what good looks like* — this is the original BE 2.0 architecture (chapters 2–9), with the 2020 additions folded in. For a founder of a small company, these six, in this order, are the operating core.

### 3.1 Leadership style — it all starts with you
> "Leadership is the art of getting people to want to do what must be done." — Jim Collins (BE 2.0, ch. 3)

Leadership has a **universal function** — catalyze a clear and shared vision and get genuine commitment to it — and a **personal style**. There is no leadership personality. Gandhi and Churchill could not be more different; both worked. What matters is that style is authentically yours. A leader's style is a **multiplier**: on a small company, it touches everyone daily, so it either energizes everything or acts as a wet blanket over everything.

**The seven elements common to effective styles** (develop each in your own way — rate yourself honestly on each):

| Element | What it means in practice |
| --- | --- |
| **Authenticity** | Live the values; be the best role model. Sam Walton flew coach and drove an old pickup — behavior consistent with the culture he demanded. Conviction is visible: show raw commitment to the values, and align major decisions with them (Chouinard killing pitons to force cleaner climbing gear). |
| **Decisiveness** | Analysis rarely settles a question by itself — combine it with judgment and intuition. On important calls, make it participative: invite disagreement *before* deciding, then unify. Accept that mistakes are part of building strength. |
| **Focus** | No more than three priorities at a time. Work is infinite; you must choose what not to do. If everything is a priority, nothing is. |
| **Personal touch** | Build real relationships (they become the company's bonds); write handwritten notes; stay accessible; use symbolic details (Debbie Fields throwing away a tray of cookies that was a quarter-inch too thin is worth more than a policy memo) — and avoid the micromanagement that personal touch can decay into. |
| **Hard + soft people skills** | Combine very high standards with genuine positive feedback. John Wooden and Bill Walsh were demanding *and* warm; it's an AND, not an OR. |
| **Communication** | The vision must be repeated constantly — J&J's Jim Burke spent about 40% of his time on the company credo. Use vivid analogies and examples; call a duck a duck (no turgid, uninspiring fog). |
| **Ever forward** | Hard work without workaholism; constant self-improvement; energy, optimism, tenacity. The leader's battery is the company's battery. |

**The M syndrome: how style kills a company.** BE 2.0 opens with the portrait of "M" — a brilliant CEO (IQ 150+, PhD, MBA) whose oppressive style stalled a promising company at ~$15M in revenue. The pattern is worth memorizing as a checklist of what to avoid: values professed but contradicted daily; indecisiveness; a flood of conflicting priorities; isolation from the team; turgid communication with no vision; constant criticism and no positive reinforcement; resistance to new ideas and resulting talent drain. Be honest — every leader drifts toward parts of this. Audit yourself regularly (Tool 3).

**Level 5 leadership is the aim.** The capability hierarchy: (1) highly capable individual, (2) contributing team member, (3) competent manager, (4) effective leader who commits people to a compelling vision, (5) **executive who builds enduring greatness through a blend of personal humility and professional will**. Level 5 leaders point out the window to give credit and look in the mirror to take responsibility. Since this was introduced in *Good to Great* but seeded in BE, treat it as the destination of your leadership development, not a fixed state.

**Decisions have a timeline.** Debate and even fight hard for your view *before* the decision; once decided, unify — even those who disagreed, fully commit. Match the process to the clock: Kennedy's team asked questions for days during the Cuban Missile Crisis because the risks would not change quickly; on 9/11 the FAA's Ben Sliney had minutes, and landed 4,556 flights with almost no debate. Ask: *how much time do we have before the risk changes?* Then decide in that timeline.

**Don't confuse empowerment with detachment.** Give people real ownership — and stay personally, visibly hands-on with your handful of strategic imperatives. When a CEO gets out of the way of everything, the company loses its center.

### 3.2 People — first who, then what
> "Great vision without great people is irrelevant." — BE 2.0, ch. 2

**Key seats, and the one number.** Not every seat is equal. A seat is **key** if (a) the person in it makes significant people decisions, (b) failure in it could expose the company to catastrophe, or (c) success in it has an outsized impact. Your number one metric as a founder: **what percentage of key seats are filled with the right people?** Target ≥ 90%. Discuss it constantly. Measure it quarterly (Tool 6).

**Develop or replace — the seven questions.** When someone in a key seat underperforms, most founders wait too long. Work through these seven honestly before deciding (Tool 7):

1. Is keeping this person causing other talented people to leave?
2. Is the issue **values** (character), **will** (drive), or **skills** (trainable)?
3. Does the person point out the window when things go wrong (blaming outside) or look in the mirror?
4. Does the person see a job or a responsibility?
5. Has your confidence in them grown or shrunk over the past year?
6. Is it a bus problem (wrong person) or a seat problem (right person, wrong role)?
7. How would you feel if the person quit tomorrow?

When the decision is made: be **rigorous, not ruthless** — move decisively, and let people exit with dignity. Later, most people who left should still feel good about the company. And autopsy your hiring mistakes so the pattern doesn't repeat.

**The trust wager.** Default to assuming people are trustworthy until incontrovertible evidence to the contrary — while never leaving yourself open to catastrophe and keeping your eyes on cash flow. Mistrust drives away exactly the best people, who feel it instantly. When trust breaks, distinguish between a well-intentioned incompetent (trainable) and someone who repeatedly abused trust deliberately (not recoverable).

**Founders can grow — and must.** "Steve Jobs 1.0 should not be confused with Steve Jobs 2.0." Anne Bakar inherited Telecare at 29 and grew through her own versions 2.0, 3.0… It is a myth that founders must be replaced by "real CEOs"; the data says many great companies were built by founders who grew alongside the company. Heirs and successors (Katharine Graham, Leon Gorman) can measure up too. Your job: keep becoming a bigger leader than you are today.

**Lead from your unit.** Don't obsess over your career; obsess over your team — excellence in the unit in front of you is what earns the next responsibility (Anne Mulcahy at Xerox; General Lloyd Austin). Make your corner a pocket of greatness.

**Motivation: money is not the mechanism.** Collins found no systematic link between financial incentives and building greatness — and found a cautionary tale (Wells Fargo's sales incentives producing accounts customers never asked for). The best people are driven by cause, pride, craft, and responsibility to peers (the Cleveland Clinic, the Navy SEALs). Pay fairly and aligned with values, to attract and keep the right people — never as the primary engine for performance.

**Build a culture of mutual commitment.** The deepest motivation is people who depend on people: crews, teams, units where members would go to extraordinary lengths for each other (William Manchester in the Pacific; Fred Smith's FedEx "absolutely, positively overnight"). Deliberately build interdependence: shared goals, public commitments, teams who train together, promises that are sacred.

### 3.3 Vision — values, purpose, and a mountain to climb
> "Vision precedes greatness, not vice versa." — BE 2.0, ch. 4

The founders of great companies instilled the vision while the company was still small and malleable — IBM, HP, Merck, L.L. Bean, Patagonia. Vision brings four benefits: a basis for extraordinary effort, a context for decisions, cohesion and community, and independence from any few key individuals.

**The framework: three parts, on three timescales.**

| Part | What it is | Timescale | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Core values & beliefs** | The handful of timeless principles you truly stand for — authentic to the leaders or they will ring hollow | Never change | Test each candidate hard (7 tests in Tool 9); if a value can be traded away when it becomes a competitive disadvantage, it was never core. Keep the list short (≤6). "Profit is a necessity, not the ultimate goal." |
| **Purpose** | The fundamental reason for existing — a guiding star to pursue, never quite reach | ~100 years | Use the five whys to dig from "we make X" to the real why (Patagonia: "to be a role model and tool for social change"). Inspiring, expansive, and it should help you decide what *not* to do. |
| **Mission / BHAG** | A clear, compelling goal with a finish line and a time frame — bold enough to make you uneasy | 10–25 years | The term BHAG (Big Hairy Audacious Goal) was born in this book. Four proven types: **targeting** ("become…"), **common enemy**, **role model**, **internal transformation**. It should excite everyone, not just executives, and be ~50–70% achievable — a zone of discomfort, not a plan. |

**Do it as a mission, not a statement.** Collins is emphatic: this is not a wordsmithing exercise and not about "mission statements." It's goal setting — picking your mountain — and the same goal should survive many wordings ("climb Mount Everest" = "climb the mountain Hillary climbed first" — the mountain matters, not the phrase). Then bring it alive with a **vivid description**: paint the scene of what it will look like when you've reached it (Sony's 1950s BHAG: to become the company most known for changing the worldwide image of "Made in Japan" — pursued with a vivid, specific, passionate picture, not a slogan).

**The Mars Group.** To write the vision, don't use only the executives. Imagine recreating your company's best attributes on Mars — but the rocket only seats 5–7 people. Who would you send? Nominations from everyone, then those most nominated distill the vision draft; leaders respond to it afterward. This is one of the most reliable ways to articulate a vision that's true.

**When the BHAG is reached, set the next one.** DPR Construction's founders held a "constitutional convention" at a winery: purpose — "We exist to build great things" — and a BHAG to become a truly great construction company by 2000. They hit huge milestones and set the next BHAG for 2030. The "we've arrived" syndrome (lavish offices, golf, coasting) is a vision killer.

**The cascade (from the official Vision Framework):** Vision → BHAG (10–25 yrs) → **5-year base-camp objectives** → strategies → tactics. If your strategy can't be traced up this chain, it's noise.

### 3.4 Strategy — a road map to the mission
> Strategy is the *methodology for achieving the current mission*. Not a feeling, not a deck — choices.

**Four principles.** Strategy (1) **descends from the vision** (it serves the mission), (2) **leverages your strengths**, (3) **is realistic** — it starts from a brutally honest assessment, and (4) **is set with the people who will carry it out**. Strategy made alone in a room and handed down doesn't get executed.

**The assessment.** Internal: strengths, weaknesses, resources, innovations. External: industry trends, technology, competitors, regulation, demographics, international threats/opportunities. The whole point is to *confront reality* — the facts, not the comfortable story.

**The essence of strategy — three questions** (spend your strategic energy here, not on slide design):

1. **Where do we place big bets?** Bets must be empirically validated — fire bullets first, then concentrate your forces on what hits (Intel's memory chips; see principle 8).
2. **How do we protect our flanks?** Keep reserves. Never leave the company exposed to catastrophe; Churchill held back 25 fighter squadrons at the fall of France, against all pressure, "to keep the margin of safety."
3. **How do we extend our victories?** When something works, turn the flywheel harder — don't let momentum dissipate (the Union let Lee's army escape after Gettysburg; don't let your wins go unfinished).

**The annual artifact: a strategy on ~3 pages, with ≤5 priorities.** Each priority gets **one name** — one person ultimately responsible. More than five and you have none. This one rule, kept annually, will outperform any elaborate planning system.

**Four growth questions every small company faces** (decide them explicitly rather than drifting):
- *How fast should we grow?* Growth eats cash; roughly half of all bankruptcies happen after a year of record sales. Fast growth is a choice to be made on purpose, tied to the vision.
- *Focus or diversify?* Focus, then phased diversification. Most companies lose their edge when they spread into unrelated businesses.
- *Should we go public?* Weigh capital badly needed against cost, scrutiny, and short-term pressure. (For many BE-style companies: probably not, unless it serves the mission specifically.)
- *Lead or follow?* Pioneer status correlates with market share (in one study of 1,853 businesses, pioneers averaged 29% share) — but best eventually beats first: execution is what protects you. Innovation is mandatory; pioneering is optional.

**And when you cannot control prices, you must control costs.** Buffett's test of a great business: the ability to raise prices without losing customers. Most small companies don't have that power — so cost discipline is a strategic weapon, not an accounting chore. Southwest and Nucor won by making low cost structural, not a temporary squeeze.

### 3.5 Innovation — a company of ideas, not a founder of ideas
> The challenge is not generating ideas; it's building an *innovative company* rather than one dependent on an innovative founder.

**Six elements of a company that keeps inventing:**

1. **Receptivity to ideas from everywhere** — inside and out. Systematically kill "wet blanket" reflexes; the telephone, FedEx, and Apple were all greeted by confident dismissal. Build channels for ideas (customers, front-line staff, suppliers) and respond fast.
2. **Be the customer** — many breakthroughs come from inventors solving their own problems. The **woodwork factor**: when you genuinely scratch your own itch, customers no market research could have found come out of the woodwork (Band-Aid, Tagamet).
3. **Experimentation and mistakes** — Edison's 9,000 attempts; PowerBar's kitchen trials. Run small projects that are easy to end; celebrate the costly-lesson-that-was-cheap. Fire bullets.
4. **People being creative** — hire some unusual people (the occasional "wild duck"), make space for design and tinkering, and train creativity like a skill (curiosity, constraints, time away from the fray).
5. **Autonomy and decentralization** — slice the diamond into small units that can experiment without asking permission; big companies kill ideas by process.
6. **Rewards** — recognition, credit, and advancement paths that let creative contributors grow without being forced into management.

**Idea push vs. market pull: you need both.** Great innovators listen to the market *and* push inventions the market hasn't imagined. And remember: **creativity is the easy part — discipline is rare.** Many companies can come up with ideas; few can scale them. The flywheel, the march, and tactical excellence are what convert invention into institution.

### 3.6 Tactical excellence — vision and strategy are empty without the moves
> Vision and strategy matter only if the details get executed — climbing El Capitan is moves, or it is nothing.

**The central tenet: poor execution is the leader's fault** (not "lazy employees" — the leader's failure to create the conditions). People execute consistently when they: **are clear** on what to do, **have the tools**, **are appreciated**, and **see why the work matters** (Drucker's aircraft-parts workers, whose morale transformed only after they met the aircrews who flew their bombers).

**The never-ending six-part process** — a loop, not a checklist:

1. **Hiring** — for values first. Skills can be taught; character at scale cannot be installed.
2. **Enculturating** — onboarding that transmits the values and standards by stories and practice, not slogans.
3. **Training** — the tools, the standards, the craft. Continuous.
4. **Goal setting** — every person, every quarter: clear goals, responsibilities (not "jobs"), one person ultimate responsibility (OPUR) per critical task.
5. **Measuring** — visible, honest numbers; process and outcomes; no surprises.
6. **Appreciating** — recognition that's real, specific, and frequent. Warmth is not weakness; coldness is data: people leave when all six of these break down.

**Values standards are absolute; performance standards are high.** Anyone who repeatedly violates values goes — regardless of results. Performance standards are demanding but treatable with training and patience. Confusing the two destroys cultures in both directions.

**SMaC: the recipe.** Build a set of practices that are **Specific, Methodical, and Consistent** — the company's recipe for results (how you source, launch, sell, deliver, review; margins you hold; behaviors you never skip). Improve it deliberately (only via disciplined after-action review — usually touching one ingredient at a time), then hold it steady. Most companies have recipes that are vague and sporadically applied; that's why SMaC, kept fixed, is a competitive advantage.

**Tactical tools that turn out bolts and nuts:**

- **Deadlines: freedom in a framework.** Give the person a framework (goal, quality bar, budget) and let them choose their method and deadline — then hold them to it, no surrender. The founder's example: a contractor promised a March 31 deadline he set himself — and hit it by 15 minutes.
- **Tactical BHAGs.** Small-scale audacious goals with a number and a by-when, achievable only with near-perfect consistency: e.g., "100 consecutive on-time briefings" (Collins' own team hit this on March 22, 2018). They make excellence visible and teachable.
- **Expectations.** Satisfaction = outcome minus expectation. Tell people the honest state of the storm (like the second pilot who announced the delay with truth and warmth), and you keep trust even through bad news.
- **OPURs.** For every critical task: One Person Ultimately Responsible. Shared responsibility is no responsibility.

**The closing foundation: respect.** Great companies respect their customers, their relationships, and above all their people — which is exactly why they expect high performance from them. The two go together: respect without standards is tolerance; standards without respect is abuse. (L.L. Bean's warehouse measured 99.89% flawless shipments in 1987 *and* resoled a customer's 30-year-old boots; both are the same culture.)

## 4. How it all fits together

**One engine, three views.** The Map (12 principles) is the *research view* — the fundamental inputs. The six areas (leadership style, people, vision, strategy, innovation, tactics) are the *founder's view* — where you actually work each week. The cascade is the *planning view* — how vision becomes daily action down and how careful execution feeds results back up.

```
VISION          core values (never change) · purpose (~100 yrs) · BHAG (10–25 yrs)
   │                     + vivid description of what it will look like
   ▼
OBJECTIVES      5-year base camp objectives on the way up the mountain
   │
   ▼
STRATEGY        ≤5 priorities, one owner each · big bets / flanks / victories · 3 pages
   │               (fire bullets first; protect reserves; extend what works)
   ▼
TACTICS         the six-part loop: hire → enculturate → train → goals → measure → appreciate
   │               SMaC recipe · 20-mile march · OPURs · deadlines · tactical BHAGs
   ▼
RESULTS         superior results · distinctive impact · lasting endurance
   │                                    ── hardens into ──
   ▼
ENDURE          clock building · a culture that outlasts any leader · the next BHAG
```

**The master duality to hold permanently in your head: preserve the core / stimulate progress.** Core values and purpose: frozen for a century. Everything else — products, channels, org charts, even strategies and BHAGs — permanently open to change. Almost every failure of a once-good company is some version of getting this backwards: changing the values to fit the moment, or freezing the practices until they rot.

**And the deepest question of all — the one this whole system exists to answer:** *Are you building a company that depends on you, or a company that would keep being great without you?* Clock building is the last mile of "beyond entrepreneurship" — and the hardest one for a founder. See `02-system.md` for the routines that make it real.

## 5. Foundations — the founder's own operating system (from Bill Lazier)

BE 2.0 opens with life lessons from Bill Lazier, Collins' mentor — because nothing built on a hollow founder lasts. These six are load-bearing:

1. **Start with values, always values.** A great company reflects the core values of its leaders; entrepreneurial success is less about what you do than who you are. Leon Bean's line: "I'm already eating three meals a day, and I couldn't eat a fourth" — enough is enough when the next dollar would cost you your principles.
2. **Make the trust wager.** Lead with trust (bounded by never risking catastrophe). If you assume people are untrustworthy, you will drive away the best ones — and you'll be right about the worst ones at a catastrophic price.
3. **Never stifle a generous impulse.** Generosity compounds: give and it returns as energy, loyalty, and relationships — a flywheel of its own (Hewlett's motto, lived by Lazier).
4. **Make the irreversible leap when it's time.** Commit fully to what you've chosen; living with one foot out the door halves your odds. ("If you spend your life keeping your options open, that's exactly what you'll do — keep your options open.")
5. **Build a meaningful life by building relationships.** In a great relationship, both parties answer "I benefit more." Mentorship is a relationship, not a transaction — and it multiplies through the people you develop.
6. **Put the butter on your waffles.** Lazier, before open-heart surgery: he'd already had a great life — everything after was a bonus. Ambition for the company, yes; but hold life itself with gratitude, and make the journey enjoyable (even the hard parts). Joy sustains the decades that greatness requires.
