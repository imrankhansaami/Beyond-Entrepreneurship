# 02 · The System — Running It

*The framework describes how an enduring great company works. This file is how you run it — the routine, the rules, and the mechanisms. Greatness comes from consistency more than intensity; this is the consistency.*

*Golden rule for everything below: **manage the system, not the people.** You don't motivate people; you recruit self-motivated ones and build an environment that doesn't demotivate them. Mechanisms beat willpower — every time.*

---

## 0. Your role: architect and clock-builder

You have two jobs, permanently:

1. **Architect of greatness** — laying the foundation *while the company is still small and malleable enough to be hand-crafted* around its values (this is the whole premise of BE: the foundation goes in early).
2. **Clock-builder** — building a company that works without you. Your success metric includes how it performs in the hands of your successor.

Everything you do in the four rhythms below should be traceable to one of these. If a meeting, ritual, or habit serves neither, cut it.

**The founder micro-operating-system (do these weekly, forever):**
- Keep **no more than three priorities** for yourself at a time.
- Practice the **window and the mirror**: credit out the window, responsibility in the mirror.
- Lead with the **trust wager**; watch for its two failure modes (naïve catastrophe exposure; punitive mistrust).
- **Lift people up**: one handwritten note, one specific appreciation, one piece of honest feedback per week.
- **Repeat the vision** in some form every single week. Say it again. Then again.
- **Ever forward**: protect your energy and optimism — the leader's battery is the company's battery — without running a workaholic's life.

## 1. The four rhythms at a glance

| Rhythm | Length | Purpose | Output |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Annual Reset** | 1.5–2 days off-site | Face reality completely; re-decide the vision, strategy, priorities, key seats, marches | One-page company plan; ≤5 priorities with owners; updated dashboard |
| **Quarterly Advance** | Half day | Score the marches; push the flywheel; decide people calls; choose bullets and cannonballs | Quarter plan; key-seat map; decisions log |
| **Monthly Truths** | 90 minutes | Hard numbers + one deep brutal fact + people check + appreciation | Updated dashboard; corrections |
| **Weekly Focus** | 45–60 minutes | Focus, OPURs, red flags, decisions, expectations | A short list of actions with one name each |

**Solo/small-team note.** If you don't yet have a leadership team, run every rhythm anyway — as your own working sessions, and later invite in the people who will carry it. The rhythm is the teacher: your team will learn the system by living it.

## 2. The Annual Reset — the anchor of the year

One off-site a year, run in two halves: **Day 1 is for reality; Day 2 is for decisions.** Never mix them — starting with decisions corrupts the facts.

### Day 1 — Reality (no decisions yet)

1. **Outputs scorecard.** Grade the three outputs honestly: superior results (financial performance + progress to purpose), distinctive impact ("if we disappeared, who would miss us and why?"), lasting endurance (could this run without the founder for a year?). Use the A–F grades and the 3-year trends (Tool 1 / dashboard).
2. **The Map, all 12 principles.** Not for scoring — for finding the two or three principles where the gap is biggest. Everyone brings their individual 12-question reflections (Tool 2) to the room.
3. **The brutal facts hour.** The session where the truth is heard: what's not working, what we're avoiding, what the customer complaints really say, what the numbers hide, what the departing employee said on the way out. Rules: autopsies *without blame*; questions more than statements; evidence, not opinion; no one gets penalized for the news they bring. (If you only keep one mechanism from this entire system, keep this one.)
4. **Key-seat audit.** Open the key-seat map: every key seat, every name, right person yes/no, trend. Compute the %. (Target ≥ 90%; below it, this is priority one for the year.)
5. **Big-bet review.** Every bullet fired last year: what did we learn? Which bullets hit and deserve a cannonball? What did we spend on cannonballs that should have been bullets?
6. **Paranoia sweep.** Walk the five stages of decline with fresh eyes: Hubris? Undisciplined pursuit of more? Denial of risk (cash, concentration, key-person)? Grasping for salvation (silver bullets)? Any drift toward irrelevance? Also: cash reserves, worst-case scenarios, red-flag review.
7. **Core review.** Re-confirm (or, rarely, uncover) core values and purpose — 15 minutes, not 2 hours. If your values list changed last year, that's a red flag worth discussing; values should be *discovered*, not voted on yearly.

### Day 2 — Decisions

1. **The mountain.** Re-state the BHAG (or set the next one if the old one is done or dead). Restate the 5-year base camp. Keep it to one sentence each; if it needs a paragraph, it's not clear yet.
2. **Strategy refresh (the three questions).** Where do we place big bets this year? What flanks must be protected (reserves, margins, key relationships, cash)? What victories do we extend (double down where the flywheel is moving)? Write the strategy on ≤3 pages — for your own files, not for an audience.
3. **The five priorities.** Choose no more than five strategic priorities for the year — the things that, if done superbly, make everything else easier or irrelevant. **One owner each** (a person, not a committee). If a sixth item appears, it must displace one of the five or wait.
4. **The marches.** Set (or re-confirm) your 20-mile march metrics: concrete, measurable, hit in good times and bad. Define what happens at each quarter's checkpoint. One or two marches is plenty for a small company.
5. **SMaC review.** Re-read your recipe — the specific, methodical, consistent practices that create your results. Change at most one ingredient this year, deliberately.
6. **The bullets list.** Choose 2–4 cheap experiments to fire next quarter (each with a budget, a deadline, and the question it answers). And name which existing bullet, if it keeps hitting, earns a cannonball this year.
7. **The stop-doing list.** Write it. Unfund, cancel, wind down. What you stop doing is as strategic as what you start.
8. **The calendar and the story.** Book all four rhythms. Then decide the communication plan: how you'll tell the company what changed, what stays, and why (use the vision language — values constant, everything else in motion).

**Output of the reset (the one-pager):** 3 outputs scorecard line · BHAG + 5-year base camp · ≤5 priorities with owners · marches + targets · bullets to fire · stop-doing list · key-seat % and the develop-or-replace decisions due this quarter.

*(Tool 19 in `03-tools.md` is the fill-in agenda pack; Tool 20 is the one-page plan.)*

## 3. Quarterly Advance — half a day

A quarter is long enough for momentum to be visible and short enough to correct course. Same agenda forever:

1. **Marches first.** Every owner reports results against their march and their priority — numbers, trend, surprises. No status vibes; actuals vs targets.
2. **Flywheel check.** In one sentence each: what pushed the flywheel this quarter? Where did we stall or lurch (a doom-loop symptom)? Is each step of our flywheel still feeding the next?
3. **Key seats, again.** Update the key-seat map, recompute the %. Any seat that changed to "no" gets the seven questions (Tool 7) and a decision **this quarter** — develop with a written plan, or replace with dignity.
4. **Bullets → Cannonballs.** Score the bullets in flight: learnings, hitters, misses (misses are cheap when they were bullets — say thank you and withdraw quietly). Decide any cannonball via the devil's advocate round (see §6).
5. **After-action on SMaC.** Three questions on the last quarter's biggest effort: *What worked? What didn't? What will we change — and what must never change?* Change at most one ingredient.
6. **Next quarter's marches and bullets.** Set the three-month checkpoints; assign owners; set deadlines.
7. **Recognition.** Specific, public, values-linked: name people who lived the values and moved the marches.
8. **Decisions log.** Write down every decision made today, by whom, by when. (It becomes the audit trail and the teaching tool.)

**Facilitation rules:** start and end on time; no phones; every claim carries evidence; disagreements are fought out *here* — the unity comes after; end with every action item having one name and a date.

## 4. Monthly Truths — 90 minutes

The monthly rhythm is where the system stays honest between quarters.

1. **Numbers (20 min).** The dashboard, one page (§9). Actuals vs targets; cash first — runway, reserve months, receivables; then marches; then the priorities' status.
2. **One brutal fact (30 min).** One topic, chosen in advance, deep-dived with data and without blame: a slipping metric, a lost customer, a competitor move, a user complaint pattern, an internal friction point. End with root causes and one action with one name. Rotate who brings it — it must not always be the founder's choice, or the truth will smell staged.
3. **People check (15 min).** Anything moving on the key-seat map? Any hiring (values-first checklist!), onboarding status, tough-love feedback that's overdue? Hiring autopsies for any recent miss — apply the lesson or it repeats.
4. **Bullets in flight (10 min).** Quick empirical read on running experiments. Kill what's dead; feed what's alive.
5. **Appreciation (10 min).** Two or three specific, sincere recognitions — tied to values and to standards, not to personality. (Do this even when — especially when — the month was hard.)
6. **Stop-doing enforcement (5 min).** Did anything on the stop list creep back? Anything new that should be stopped?

## 5. Weekly Focus — 45–60 minutes, same day, same hour

A standing weekly session (or the first hour of Monday). Keep it rigid in shape, light in feel.

1. **Focus check (10 min).** The five annual priorities: on track / off track / needs a decision. Each person names their ≤3 priorities for the week. If the week's list disagrees with the annual list, someone says so.
2. **OPUR check (5 min).** For every critical task in motion: is there One Person Ultimately Responsible, named, with a date? Shared ownership is the most common way small companies stall.
3. **Red flags (10 min).** What surprised us or worried us? Bring bad news early — that's the culture you're paying for: bad news travels up fast and without punishment.
4. **Decisions (15 min).** Anything needing a call: decide if the timeline demands it (see §6); otherwise assign a recommendation owner and a decision date (decision debt is real debt).
5. **Expectations (5 min).** What must people outside this room know this week — good or bad — and how will we tell them, honestly and warmly?
6. **Close (5 min).** One appreciation; one commitment check (last week's items, honestly).

**The founder's 15-minute weekly self-review** (private, non-negotiable): Where did I practice window-and-mirror this week? Did I repeat the vision? Did I stay in my three priorities? Did something I did contradict the values — and will I correct it out loud?

## 6. The decision system

**Before the decision — participative by design.**
- For any important call: invite disagreement *first*, and mean it. Debate in search of the best answer, not to win. The strongest idea wins, whoever brought it.
- Match the process to the clock. Ask: *how much time do we have before the risk changes?* Days (Kennedy's missile-crisis questions)? Hours (a crisis response)? Minutes (Sliney landing 4,556 flights on 9/11)? Choose the speed the situation demands — deliberation poorly timed is also a failure.
- Cannonballs need a devil's advocate round: one named person argues the strongest case against, on the record.

**The decision rules.**
- Once decided, **unify**: even those who disagreed commit fully. (Disagree-and-commit is not optional — it's the price of having been heard.)
- The decider can be pushed up the timeline, never down: if you'd bet the company, it's yours; otherwise delegate with an owner and a date.
- **High stakes, unclear facts →** fire a bullet instead of deciding blind (test cheaply first).
- **Recurring small decisions →** make a standing rule or a boundary ("freedom within a framework"), so the system decides, not the meeting.
- Deadlines are commitments: person sets their own within a framework (goal, standards, budget) — then no surrender without renegotiation *before* the deadline.

**After the decision.**
- Keep a **decisions journal** (Tool 18): decision, date, owner, reasoning, expected outcome, review date. Review the misses — that's how judgment compounds.
- Window/mirror on every outcome: credit outward, responsibility inward.

## 7. The people system

People decisions are the highest-leverage decisions you make. Run them as a system, not as moods.

### The loop (never ends)

1. **Hire — values first, rigorously.** Use at least three evaluation devices per candidate (multiple interviews with different lenses, references you actually call, background/work sampling). Interview for values (Tool 8); skills can be taught, character cannot be installed. **When in doubt, don't hire** — keep the seat open and carry the load until the right person appears. Skill for a key seat, values for every seat.
2. **Enculturate.** Onboarding is a values transmission ritual: stories, standards, why the work matters, who we are at our best and worst. New people should meet the culture, not the org chart.
3. **Train.** Constantly. Craft, tools, safety (of all kinds). Training is an investment in the flywheel, not a cost.
4. **Goal setting.** Every person, every quarter: a few clear goals; duties framed as *responsibilities*, not jobs; OPUR on every critical task. No orphan tasks.
5. **Measure.** Visible, honest, consistent. People can't hit standards they can't see. (And measure the right things — a warehouse that measures shipments flawlessly at 99.89% is a culture, not a metric. But they'd tell you the metric built it.)
6. **Appreciate.** Specific, sincere, values-linked, frequent. Recognition is oxygen; most small companies suffocate people quietly and wonder why they leave.

### Key seats — the permanent process

- Keep a written key-seat map (Tool 6). **Target: 90%+ filled with the right people.** Below 90%, it's your #1 topic.
- Recalibrate **quarterly** — people change, roles change, and "right person" is a moving judgment. Both directions: someone slipping, or someone who has grown into more.
- For any doubt: the **seven questions** (Tool 7): costing us other people? values/will/skills? window or mirror? job or responsibility? confidence trend? bus or seat problem? how would you feel if they quit?
- **Rigor, not ruthlessness:** move decisively, exit with dignity, keep relationships. The people who leave should still be your ambassadors.
- **Autopsy every hiring mistake**: what signal did we misread? Add a check to the process. Over years, your people decisions should *measurably* improve.

### Culture of mutual commitment

Deliberately build interdependence — the deepest fuel we found in the research:
- Public commitments with witnesses (weekly huddles, quarterly promises).
- Teams that train, struggle, and win *together* (cross-functional projects; paired goals; shared bonuses for shared outcomes — carefully, see below).
- Promises treated as sacred: leadership keeps every commitment, visibly, or renegotiates in the open long before the deadline.
- Respect first: high performance and deep respect are one culture, not two. Standards without respect is abuse; respect without standards is abandonment.

### Compensation and incentives — read this twice

There is no evidence that financial incentives *build* greatness; there is abundant evidence they can destroy it (Wells Fargo's sales machine). Therefore:
- Pay fairly, generously at the top of your range for the right people — for **attracting and retaining** them.
- Keep variable incentives small, few, and aligned with values and long-term outcomes — never with volume alone.
- Compensate for values-aligned behavior and contributions, not for muscle-flexing numbers. When someone asks "what's in it for me?" — the promised answer is *meaning, growth, fair pay, and a team that would go through a wall for each other*. If they need money to be motivated, they may be in the wrong seat.

## 8. The communication system

**Vision repetition is the core mechanism.** J&J's Jim Burke spent ~40% of his time on the credo. Your cadence:
- **Weekly**: find one moment (huddle, one-on-one, email) to connect today's work to the vision.
- **Monthly**: a story at all-hands — a person who lived a value, a customer saved, a bullet learnings report. Stories travel where slogans die.
- **Quarterly**: restate the flywheel, the marches, the BHAG progress in plain language. Numbers + meaning.
- **Annually**: re-articulate the vivid description — what it will look like when we get there — with fresh specifics.

**Techniques that work:**
- Vivid analogies and concrete images, not abstractions. Paint the picture.
- **Call a duck a duck.** Plain, direct, turgid-free language — in good news and bad.
- **Expectations out loud.** In a storm (real or financial), tell people the truth with warmth: what's happening, what it means, what happens next. Satisfaction = outcome − expectation; honesty is the cheapest morale tool in existence.
- **Questions over statements.** In any review, ask more than you announce — the leader's statements end the search for truth; the leader's questions start it.
- **Celebrate the messengers.** The person who brings bad news early is your best asset; treat them accordingly, publicly.

**The videotape test.** If every conversation, decision, and action for a month were videotaped and reviewed — would observers be struck by how consistently you live your values? (That's the standard; calibrate to it.)

## 9. The dashboard — one page, updated monthly

What gets measured gets managed; what gets *discussed forever* gets done. Ten numbers, one page (Dashboard tab in `be-dashboard.html` or Tool 21):

| # | Metric | Definition / target | Rhythm |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Key seats filled with right people | % of key seats · **target ≥ 90%** | Monthly glance; quarterly recalibrate |
| 2 | Cash runway & reserve months | Months of expenses covered · paranoia standard: never open to catastrophe | Monthly, first thing |
| 3 | Profit per X (economic denominator) | Your Hedgehog denominator (per customer/store/route/project…) | Monthly |
| 4 | Marches | Actual vs target, and the **streak** (did we hold the march?) | Monthly |
| 5 | Priorities status | The ≤5 annual priorities: on track / at risk / done | Weekly glance; monthly formal |
| 6 | BHAG / base-camp milestone | This year's milestone on the 5-year camp path: % or date | Quarterly |
| 7 | Bullets in flight | Experiments running this quarter + their questions | Monthly |
| 8 | Impact metric | The customer outcome your purpose promises ("who would miss us") | Monthly or quarterly |
| 9 | Values incidents | Count + handling of value breaches (target: 100% confronted; pattern audit) | Monthly |
| 10 | Founder-dependency | Weeks the founder could be fully away; successors being grown per key role | Quarterly |

**Reading the dashboard:** every red item gets an owner, a next action, and a date — in the same meeting. An unowned red is a lie you're telling yourself.

## 10. Mechanisms catalog — the permanent machinery

Mechanisms are the difference between a company that *depends on a person's vigilance* and one that *cannot ignore the truth*. Stand up each of these as soon as you can; each one outworks any amount of good intentions:

| Mechanism | Purpose | Cadence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Autopsies without blame | Find root causes without punishing truth-tellers | Every failure of consequence |
| Red-flag mechanism | Anyone can raise a red flag that *must* reach the founder and be answered | Continuous |
| The Council | 3–5 outside advisors who see the real numbers and tell you the brutal facts | Quarterly |
| Decision journal | Compound judgment; audit reasoning | Continuous |
| Key-seat map + recalibration | Keep the people system honest | Quarterly |
| Hiring / firing autopsies | Fix the process, not just the instance | Per event |
| After-action review (3 questions) | Deliberate improvement of SMaC | After every significant effort |
| Stop-doing list | Protect focus; prevent creep | Annual + enforced monthly |
| Mars group for vision | Authentic vision beyond the executive echo chamber | Per vision cycle |
| OPUR register | One name per critical task, always | Weekly check |
| 20-mile march scoreboard | Self-control in chaos, restraint in boom | Monthly |
| Vivid descriptions | Keep the destination emotionally alive | Annual refresh |

## 11. Failure modes — and the counter in this system

| Failure mode | How it shows up in a small company | Counter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| M syndrome (destructive style) | Contradicted values, indecision, conflicting priorities, isolation, criticism without warmth | Style audit (Tool 3); the four rhythms keep you out of the bunker |
| Doom loop | New program every quarter; lurching; rebranding as strategy | Flywheel discipline; quarterly flywheel check; stop-doing list |
| Five stages of decline | Hubris after a good year; undisciplined expansion; ignoring cash warnings; silver-bullet buys; fade | Paranoia sweep (Tool 4); cash first in the dashboard; red-flag mechanism |
| March breaks | Sprint/stall cycles; abandoning standards in busy season | March scoreboard; "in good times and bad" as the definition |
| Key-seat rot | Loyalty keeping the wrong person in a critical seat too long | 90% metric; 7 questions; rigor not ruthlessness |
| Founder dependency | Every decision routes to you; you can't be away two weeks | Clock-building tests on the dashboard; OPURs; delegating decisions with boundaries |
| Values drift | "Just this once" deals that violate stated values; nobody notices | Absolute values standards; values incidents metric; videotape test |
| Arrival syndrome | The BHAG is met; energy drains; golf enters the calendar | Set the next BHAG before the last one is celebrated |
| Financial incentive disease | Volume-based comp quietly buying the wrong behaviors | §7 compensation rules |

## 12. Installing the system — the minimum viable version

If you install only three things, install: **the weekly Focus session, the monthly Truths session, and the Annual Reset.** Add the Quarterly Advance the moment you have people to sit in it. Everything else is refinement.

- **Write the standing appointment** for each rhythm in the calendar *now* — same day, same hour, for the next 12 months.
- **Keep the artifacts** (one-page plan, key-seat map, march scoreboard, decisions log) in one folder — physical or digital. Institutional memory beats everyone's memory.
- **Teach the system as you live it.** Your team learns the flywheel by watching you refuse to lurch; they learn the values by watching you lose a deal over one.
- **Review the system itself once a year** inside the Annual Reset: which mechanism actually worked? Cut the dead ones. The system serves the company — not the other way around.

The company you are trying to build is one that would keep getting better if you were hit by a bus tomorrow. Every rhythm above exists, ultimately, to make that true.
